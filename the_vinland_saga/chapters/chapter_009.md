## Chapter 9: The Argument in the Longhouse

The smoke in the longhouse hung low, a grey shroud that tasted of peat and old grease. It clung to the rafters and stung the eyes of those who sat closest to the central fire. Outside, the wind had begun to lose its bite, shifting from a sharp blade to a dull ache, but inside, the air was thick with a different kind of heat—the friction of voices raised against the looming thaw.

Hallvard sat on a bench of hewn oak, his hands resting on the ledger. The leather cover was cracked and stained with salt spray, its edges softened by years of use. He felt the weight of it, not just in his palms but in his chest. It was the book of truth. In a land where the horizon kept shifting and the trees grew tall enough to swallow the sun, the ledger was the only thing that stayed still.

“The timber is the finest we have seen since the first landing,” Bjorn said, his voice gravelly from months of shouting over the wind. He stood near the fire, his face smeared with soot. He gestured toward the heavy logs stacked against the wall—wood so dense it rang like iron when struck. “We could build a permanent hall here. We could clear the ridge and plant-side. Why go back to the old ships? Why risk the sea when the land is opening up for us?”

A murmur of agreement rippled through the men. They were tired. Their wool tunics were heavy with the grime of winter, and their joints ached from the constant labor of survival. To them, the idea of staying—of carving a life into this soil—was a seductive promise of rest.

Hallvard didn't look up at Bjorn. He looked at the columns of figures he had spent months meticulously updating. 

“The timber is good,” Hallvard said. His voice was quiet, but it carried. “It is the best wood in the world. It will hold a roof against a gale.”

He opened the ledger to the final page of the winter tally. He cleared his throat, the sound sharp in the sudden silence of the room. 

“We have three barrels of salted cod remaining,” he began, reading plainly. “The grain stores are at half-measure, and the tallow is low enough that we cannot grease the oars for more than a month’s voyage without thinning it with fat. We have enough to see the spring through, yes. But if we stay to build, if we stay to farm this ridge, we will be eating boiled leather by the time the first frost returns.”

He turned the page. The numbers were cold and unyielding. They did not care about the quality of the timber or the beauty of the valley. 

“To stay is to gamble on a harvest we haven't planted yet,” Hallvard continued, his eyes scanning the ink. “To leave is to carry what we have and find the next shore. We are a ship’s crew, not a village. If we remain here, we are waiting for the land to provide. If we sail, we take what we have with us.”

The room went still. The fire popped, sending a spray of sparks against the soot-stained walls. Hallvard felt Eirik’s gaze on him from across the flames. Eirik sat in the shadows, his face unreadable, the weight of command etched into the deep lines around his mouth. 

In the corner, the strangers sat together. They were wrapped in furs that smelled of woodsmoke and earth. They had watched the argument with a steady, piercing intensity. They did not speak as the Norse men spoke; they moved with a deliberate economy of motion. When Hallvard finished reading the last line of the ledger, he looked at them.

A man among them—one who had spent many nights watching the stars with the crew—leaned forward. He looked at the remaining supplies, then at the heavy timber, and finally at the men who were deciding their fate. 

He spoke a single word in his own tongue. It was a sound like stones grinding together.

The Norse men looked at one another. They didn't know what it meant. They had no word for the specific weight of his question—a query about whether they intended to stay as guests or leave as ghosts. The silence that followed was heavy, filled with the things that couldn't be translated into any language.

The tension broke when Bjorn slumped back onto his bench. He looked at the ledger, then at his own calloused hands. “The math doesn't lie,” he muttered. It wasn't a surrender; it was an acknowledgment of the harsh reality Hallvard had laid bare.

“We leave,” Eirik said. The words were final. They didn't need to be shouted. 

A long, hollow silence followed—the sound of men accepting a hard truth. Then came the movement of hands. 

It began with Bjorn. He stood and approached the strangers. From his pack, he pulled a piece of seasoned wood—a block of the very timber they had argued over, shaped into a sturdy handle, polished until it shone like amber. He held it out.

The stranger took it. His fingers were long and steady. In return, he reached into a pouch at his belt and produced a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth. He handed it to Bjorn. 

Neither man spoke of what the items represented. There were no names for these exchanges in the shared vocabulary of the camp. They were simply pieces of value traded between two people who understood that survival required more than just food; it required a recognition of one another’s existence.

Hallvard watched this exchange with a strange ache in his chest. He felt like an observer of a ritual he didn't fully comprehend, yet he understood the gravity of it perfectly.

As the men began to move about, preparing to strike the camp and gather what could be carried, Eirik stood and walked toward Hallvard. He stopped just outside the fire’s reach. 

Between them lay the knotted cord—the one Hallvard had carried since the first week of winter. It was a tangled mess of hemp and twine, something they had both touched but never discussed. 

Eirik reached down and picked it up. He held it for a moment, his thumb tracing the complex series of knots. 

“It is a tally,” Eirik said softly. His voice was thick with the exhaustion of the season. “Each knot is a day we survived when the ice tried to crack the hull. Each knot is a meal we shared when there was only enough for half the men.”

He handed it back to Hallvard. 

“It’s too late to use it as a map,” Eirik added, a ghost of a grim smile touching his lips. “But it's good to know what we did.”

Hallvard took the cord. The reconciliation wasn't in words; it was in the shared weight of the history they had just written into that ledger. They were leaving this place, and the land would eventually forget their names, but the knots remained.

Outside, the wind shifted again, carrying the scent of salt and moving water. The thaw had begun. 

Hallvard looked at the last page of his ledger—the one he had left unfinished. He picked up his charcoal and made a single mark: *Departure.*
