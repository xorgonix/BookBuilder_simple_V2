## Chapter 3: Leaving the Known Water

The last of the jagged teeth—the gray rocks that had marked the coast of Greenland—vanished into the mist. It did not happen with a splash or a roar; it simply dissolved, swallowed by a white shroud that tasted of salt and old ice. 

The jokes stopped then. They died as quickly as the land had. On the deck of the *Sif*, the laughter of the previous three nights curdled into a heavy, watchful silence. Men who had spent the evening boasting of their kills or their wives' hair now stared at the horizon with eyes narrowed against the haze. 

Hallvard stood by the mainmast, his hands calloused and stained with the dark residue of tar. He felt the ship’s pulse beneath his boots—the rhythmic groan of the clinker-built hull as it pushed through the swell. It was a steady heartbeat, but today it felt frantic.

"The fog is thickening," someone muttered. 

It was a low voice, barely audible over the slap of water against the hull, but in the silence, it carried like a bell. Hallvard didn't answer. He watched a spray of foam crest over the bow, white and biting. The sea here was different than the coastal waters; it was deeper, colder, and lacked the predictable rhythm of the shallows.

He turned his attention to the water cask. It sat heavy in its cradle, the wood swollen with moisture. His job was to keep them alive, to ensure that every drop was accounted for. He watched a man—a broad-shouldered oarsman named Torsten—reach toward the bucket. 

"Not yet," Hallvard said. His voice was gravelly, used only when necessary. "The morning ration is for after the mid-watch."

Torsten’s face tightened. "My throat feels like it’s been rubbed with sand, Hallvard."

"Then swallow your spit and wait. We are three days from the next known waypoint. If we drink now, we have two less by the time the mist breaks."

The man grumbled but pulled his hand back. That was the way of things now. The luxury of abundance had stayed behind in the fjords. Here, everything was measured: the length of a rope, the weight of a loaf, the amount of water left in the belly of the ship.

By the turn of the watch, the tension shifted from the water to the quarters. 

The *Sif* was cramped. With twelve men and three boys packed into the hold, every inch of dry space was a commodity more precious than silver. A shouting match erupted near the stern—a dispute over a patch of timber that stayed relatively dry even when the spray came over the gunwales.

"It’s my turn for the high berth!" a man named Sigurd barked. He was swaying with the roll of the ship, his face flushed. "I hauled the heaviest lines during the gale."

"You slept there three nights running," another countered, shoving Sigurd's shoulder. "Move back, or I’ll move you."

The argument escalated, voices rising into a jagged cacophony that threatened to wake the sleeping. Hallvard moved. He didn't run; he walked with a deliberate, heavy step that commanded space. He stepped between them, his presence a wall of wool and grim intent.

"Enough," Hallvard said. 

The men fell silent. They looked at him—the steward, the man who knew where their food came from and how much water remained in the cask.

Hallvard looked at Sigurd, then at the man opposite him. He saw the exhaustion in their eyes, the way their shoulders slumped. The ship was a pressure cooker, and they were all looking for an escape from the damp.

"Sigurd, you take the berth," Hallvard commanded. "And you," he pointed to the other man, "you take the floor space by the mast. It’s damp, but it’s yours."

"What about your spot?" Sigurd asked, his voice still trembling with leftover adrenaline.

Hallvard looked at his own pallet—a meager arrangement of furs and wool that was currently soaked through from a leak in the overhead seam. He felt the cold seep into his bones. 

"I'll take the floor," Hallvard said. "Move."

They moved. The silence returned, heavier than before. Hallvard sat down on the hard timber, the dampness soaking into his tunic. It was a small sacrifice of comfort, but he knew that if one man felt cheated, the whole crew would begin to fray. A frayed crew was a dead ship.

He sat for a moment in the gloom before a shadow moved near him. 

It was Ketil, the youngest of the boys. He was barely sixteen, with skin that hadn't yet lost its childhood roundness and hands that still shook when they grew cold. The boy was huddled against the bulkhead, trying to make himself small.

"Hallvard?" he whispered.

"Speak."

"The men... they talk about what happens if we fall over the side. If the sea takes us." Ketil’s voice broke on the last word. He looked up at Hallvard, his eyes wide and shimmering with a terror that hadn't been suppressed by the routine of the voyage. "I can’t swim, Hallvard. I tried in the river back home, but I sink like a stone. I don't know how to find the top."

The admission hung in the air, thick and suffocating. In the Norse world, a man who could not swim was a liability—a weight that might pull others down. If the captain knew, Ketil might be relegated to the deepest part of the hold, or worse, he might be seen as unfit for the voyage entirely.

Hallvard looked at the boy. He saw the fear, but he also saw the honesty. It was a heavy thing to carry—a secret that could get a boy cast out or left behind. 

"Keep your head down," Hallvard said softly. He didn't offer a platitude; there were no easy words for the sea. "Stay near the center of the ship. Don't go near the gunwales when the swells are high."

Ketil exhaled, a long, shuddering breath. "You won't tell them?"

"I told you nothing," Hallvard lied. It was a lie that felt like lead in his mouth. 

He looked away from the boy. He would keep this secret. Ketil would be his responsibility now—a debt of protection. If the boy fell, it would be on Hallvard’s head. He watched the boy retreat into the shadows of the hold, and for the first time in many years, Hallvard felt a pang of something that wasn't duty. It was pity.

The next morning brought a change in the air. The fog didn't lift, but it thinned, turning from a wall of white to a translucent veil of gray. 

Eirik stood at the bow, his face etched with lines of concentration. He was a man of few words, often dismissed by the others as a dreamer because he spent too much time watching the horizon and not enough time hauling rope. But today, he moved with a sudden, sharp purpose.

He squinted into the gray. "Birds," he said.

The crew gathered, their curiosity overriding their exhaustion. 

"What kind?" someone asked.

Eirik didn't answer immediately. He watched a small cluster of shapes moving against the wind. They were erratic, dipping and rising in the haze. He waited until they disappeared before turning back to the men.

"They are heading south-southwest," Eirik said. His voice was flat, devoid of the pride that usually accompanied such claims. 

He took a hand-line—a length of rope with a lead weight—and cast it into the water. He let it drop, watching the tension in the line. He didn't look at the depth; he looked at the way the birds behaved as they moved toward the land that wasn't there yet.

"How do you know?" Sigurd asked, leaning forward. "You can barely see them."

Eirik looked at him. His eyes were like flint. "The wind carries their scent. The water tells me where they rest."

"Is it a bearing?" another man asked. "Give us a number."

Eirik tightened his grip on the line. He didn't give a number. He didn't give a direction beyond the vague movement of the birds. He simply stood there, a sentinel in the mist, refusing to explain the mechanics of his intuition. 

He knew something they did not—that the land was close enough for the birds to find it, but far enough that the sea still held its teeth.

Hallvard watched Eirik from the stern. He saw the way the man’s hands trembled slightly as he pulled the line back up. It wasn't fear; it was the strain of holding a secret knowledge against the weight of the unknown. 

The ship groaned, turning its nose toward the direction Eirik had indicated. The fog remained, but for the first time in six nights, there was a flicker of something other than dread in the men’s eyes. It wasn't hope—hope was for those on land. It was merely the grim determination of those who still had a path to follow.

Hallvard turned back to his cask. He gripped the handle, feeling the cold moisture of the wood. They were moving forward. Whether they were moving toward life or the bottom of the sea remained to be seen.
