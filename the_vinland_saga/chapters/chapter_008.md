## Chapter 8: What the Guests Knew

The wind did not merely blow; it sought out the seams of the world, prying into the gaps between the timber walls and the heavy hides draped over the doors. It carried the scent of old snow and the sharp, metallic tang of deep frost.

Hallvard sat on a low stool, his fingers aching in a way that felt permanent. He was a steward by trade, a man whose life was measured in sacks of grain, barrels of salt pork, and the steady tally of what remained versus what was needed to see another week of winter. In the dim light of the central fire, the faces of the crew were etched in grey—shadows of men who had seen their stores dwindle and their bodies grow heavy with the lethargy of the cold.

Two of the strangers remained. They had stayed when the others had retreated into the inner sanctum of the longhouse or huddled in their furs. They sat across from Hallvard, their presence a quiet weight in the room. They did not speak much Norse, and Hallvard’s own tongue was clumsy when trying to shape the sounds they made, but work required no common vocabulary.

Between them lay a heap of tangled netting—the heavy hempen mesh used for the larger catches.

Hallvard took a length of twine, his hands moving with the rhythmic precision of a man who had spent twenty years on deck. *Loop. Pull. Knot.* Beside him, one of the strangers did the same. The man was older, his face a map of deep creases, his hair the color of bleached bone. He worked with a steady hand that didn't tremble, even as the fire crackled and threw sparks against the rafters.

"The tide," the stranger said suddenly. His voice was low, a gravelly rasp that seemed to come from deep in his chest. He pointed toward the heavy door, where the wind howled. "It moves before the moon is full."

Hallvard paused, his needle hovering over a tear in the mesh. He looked at the man. To the Norsemen, the tide was a thing of fate—a cycle to be observed and endured. They knew when it turned, but they did not know *why* or how to read its pulse beyond what the sea gave them.

The stranger reached into a pouch at his belt and pulled out a strip of cured leather. On it were markings—not runes, nor letters, but a series of precise notches and shaded lines. He pointed to a section and then toward the bay outside. 

"We know the water’s path," the man said in his broken tongue. "The fish come when the water pushes into the rocks. We do not hunt where they are not."

Hallvard looked at the leather, then back at the net. He realized with a jolt of clarity that these men weren't just surviving; they were calculating. They had tables for the sea’s movements. While the Norsemen relied on the strength of their arms and the luck of their eyes, these people lived by a measured understanding of the water's rhythm. 

He looked at his own hands—calloused, scarred, but ultimately guessing at the sea's moods. He felt a strange prickle of humility. They were guests in his camp, yet they possessed a map of the invisible.

A few hours passed in the shared silence of labor. The fire burned down to glowing embers, and the air grew thick with the smell of wet wool and woodsmoke. 

"The landing," the stranger said again, his eyes fixed on the flickering orange light. He gestured toward the direction of the shore where they had first struck their ships. "The fire-pit you found. It is not a grave for old fires."

Hallvard frowned. He remembered the site—a circle of stones, blackened and smoothed by years of use. They had assumed it was a relic of some long-dead tribe, a place where someone had once sat to keep the night at bay. 

"It is a signal," the man said. He leaned forward, his face illuminated by the dying fire. "When the inland people move, they look for the spark. It tells them where the water meets the land. It tells them who stays."

The realization hit Hallvard like a physical blow. The site wasn't a piece of history; it was a piece of infrastructure. It meant there were others—not just these two men, but a network of people moving through the interior, watching the coast, communicating across distances that stretched beyond the sight of the horizon.

The door creaked open, admitting a blast of air so cold it made the fire hiss. Eirik stood in the entrance, his heavy cloak dusted with white. He looked older than he had a week ago, the weight of command carving deeper lines into his brow. 

He didn't come to join them at the fire. He walked straight to the table where the supplies were kept and pulled out a piece of parchment—the chart they had used to navigate the coastline.

"The others are moving," Eirik said, his voice barely above a whisper. "The scouts who went inland... they returned with something."

He held up a small bundle of cord. It was made of some fibrous plant material, not hemp or wool. It was tied with a series of complex knots—loops and hitches that defied Hallvard’s understanding of maritime rope-work. 

"It came from the interior," Eirik said. "The strangers say it is a message."

Hallvard looked at the knots. They were beautiful in their intricacy, yet they held no meaning for him. He felt like a man looking at a locked door without a key. 

"Who sent it?" Hallvard asked.

Eirik sighed, a long, weary sound. He laid the chart flat on the table. "The strangers say there is a third group. Further inland. They are not like the ones we saw near the river. They do not trade. They do not wait."

He pointed to the margin of the chart—the vast, empty space where the ink stopped and the parchment remained white. 

"I told you this voyage was limited," Eirik said, his voice heavy with a sudden, grim honesty. "The chart ends here because we do not know what lies beyond the next ridge. We have sailed into a room with no windows, Hallvard. These knots... they are a warning that someone else is in the room with us."

Hallvard looked at the knotted cord again. He thought of the stranger’s tide tables and the signal fire. The world was much larger than their ship, and much more crowded than they had dared to imagine. 

The wind screamed against the walls, a lonely, mourning sound. Outside, the winter held its breath, waiting for the next move. Hallvard gripped his needle until his knuckles turned white, the silence of the room heavier than the cold.
