## Chapter 2: The Weight of Rope

The tar smelled of scorched earth and old blood. It clung to Hallvard’s skin, a sticky film that no amount of seawater could truly wash away. He stood in the shadow of the hull, his hands stained black, watching the shipwright hammer a timber into place. Every strike echoed against the ribs of the *knarr*, a hollow thrum that vibrated in Hallvard’s teeth.

"It won't hold," the shipwright grunted. He was a man whose face had been etched into a map of deep lines by forty years of salt air. "The wood is too green. It’ll split before we hit the first gale."

Hallvard didn't argue. He reached into his tunic and pulled out a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth. He unwrapped it to reveal a knife. The hilt was carved from whalebone, smoothed by years of use until it felt like a part of the hand that held it. It had belonged to his father—a man who had lived by the edge of such steel.

"I need the rudder pin," Hallvard said. His voice was flat, stripped of any plea. "The iron one from the forge."

The shipwright took the knife. He weighed it in his hand, his thumb tracing the blade’s edge. He looked at the *knarr*, then back at Hallvard. A long silence stretched between them, filled only by the rhythmic *thwack* of hammers nearby. 

"Your father's blade," the shipwright muttered. "A heavy price for a piece of iron."

"The ship needs to steer," Hallvard replied. "The pin is more important than the steel."

He watched the man move toward the storehouse, the knife disappearing into his belt. Hallvard felt a strange lightness in his tunic where the weight of the blade had lived for months. It was a trade of history for utility. He was stripping away the past to ensure there would be a future at all.

***

The burial happened three days later, in the mud-slicked corner of the launching yard. 

There were no prayers. The wind was too sharp for them, whipping salt spray into their eyes and biting at their cheeks. They dug a shallow trench in the earth that sat between the tide's reach and the settlement’s walls. 

The man inside—Torsten—had died of a lung-fever that had come on as quickly as it had vanished. He hadn't been a hero. He hadn't died in a raid or a storm. He had simply stopped breathing while they were hauling timber, his body collapsing into the slush like a sack of grain.

"He won't be making the voyage," someone muttered. It wasn't an observation; it was a fact as cold as the ground.

Hallvard stood at the edge of the trench. He watched as they lowered the body. There was no ornate casket, only a shroud of coarse wool. They threw a few stones over the mound and kicked the dirt back into place. It was a practical burial—a way to clear the space for those who remained. 

He looked at his hands. The tar was gone, replaced by the grey grime of the yard. He felt the absence of the knife in his pocket more than he expected. Torsten was gone, and the blade was gone, and the ship was still sitting there, waiting to be filled with enough supplies to keep them from starving before they saw land.

***

The *knarr* was finally ready for the crew. It sat in the harbor like a sleeping beast, its clinker-built sides heavy with the weight of salted meat, grain sacks, and wool. 

Inside the hull, the air was thick with the smell of damp wood and the musk of thirty men packed into a space designed for survival, not comfort. Hallvard walked the narrow gangway, his ledger in hand. He was the steward; he was the one who decided who slept where and who ate when.

"I’m not sleeping on the floor with the bilge-rats," a man named Kolur spat. He was a broad-shouldered man with a beard matted with grease. "I've got a wife in the settlement. I won't have my back aching from the damp all night."

Hallvard looked at him, his expression impassive. "The floor is where the dryest wool is kept, Kolur. You sleep where the others sleep. We divide into watches. You’ll have four hours of rest before you’re on the oars again."

"Four hours?" Kolur stepped forward, his chest heaving. "That's barely enough to dream."

"It's what we have," Hallvard said. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to. The authority of the stores was his shield. If they didn't follow the watch, the grain would run out before they saw a single coastline. 

He moved down the line, settling disputes over sleeping sacks and the placement of water barrels. He watched the men—tired professionals who were already beginning to resent the geometry of their new lives. They were being compressed into a singular machine of survival. Every man was a gear; if one jammed, the ship would founder.

Eirik appeared at the stern, his silhouette framed against the grey sky. He didn't look like a king or a conqueror. He looked like a man who knew exactly how much a human life was worth when weighed against a gallon of fresh water.

The crew fell silent as he stepped onto the deck. 

"Listen," Eirik said. His voice carried, low and steady, cutting through the wind. "We go to Vinland not to build an empire in a day. We go to live. And to live, we must have rules for when we touch those shores."

He held up three fingers.

"First: No taking. What is theirs is theirs. We take only what we need to survive, and we take it with permission where possible. We are not raiders today."

A murmur went through the men. Some looked surprised; others looked skeptical. 

"Second," Eirik continued, "No chasing. If they run, let them run. We do not hunt people like wolves hunt deer. We seek a home, not a massacre."

He paused, his eyes scanning the faces of the crew. He saw the hunger in their eyes—the hunger for land, for safety, for something more than the dying timber of Greenland.

"Third: No burning. We do not set fire to what we intend to inhabit. A burned forest gives no shelter. A burned village gives no neighbors."

He lowered his hand. 

"These are the rules. You break them, you answer to me. You follow them, and perhaps we all see the next winter."

Hallvard watched Eirik speak, then looked back at the men. He saw the tension in their jaws, the way they gripped the ropes of the railing. They were being asked to hold back their instincts—to temper their greed with a discipline that felt like another weight to carry. 

He turned back to his ledger. The voyage was no longer a dream of gold and glory. It was a matter of salt, rope, and the terrifying precision of staying alive.
