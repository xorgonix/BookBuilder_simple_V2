# The Vinland Saga

## Chapter 1: Timber and Salt

The frost had teeth. It bit into the knuckles of Hallvard’s hands as he gripped the charcoal stick, the wood of the ledger feeling slick beneath his thumb. In the warehouse at Brusand, the air tasted of old dust and the lingering, ghost-scent of resin. 

He moved between the racks, his boots crunching on floor-sweepings. He reached for a beam—a sturdy piece of oak that should have been there to support the weight of the winter stores. His hand met only empty air. 

Hallvard paused. He looked at the rack, then back at the ledger. The numbers were wrong. They had been wrong for three months, a slow bleeding of resources that no one wanted to name aloud. Greenland was a land of subtraction; every year, there was less wood, less meat, and less hope left in the marrow of the colony’s bones.

He dipped the charcoal again. He did not write what he saw. He wrote what the colony needed to believe: *Twelve beams remaining.*

A lie was a heavy thing to carry, but a truth that sparked a riot was heavier still. If the men knew the timber had run dry, they would see the end of their world in the empty racks. Hallvard preferred the slow rot of a secret to the sudden fire of a panic.

"The wood is thinning, Hallvard."

The voice came from the doorway. It was low, rasping like stones grinding together. 

Hallvard did not turn immediately. He finished his stroke before acknowledging his foster-father. Eirik stood in the shadow of the lintel, his frame draped in a heavy wool cloak that had seen too many seasons and too much salt. 

"It is thinning," Hallvard replied. "The frost took the inland growth. The blight took the rest."

Eirik stepped into the light. He didn't look at the racks. He looked at the ledger. His face was a map of deep-set lines, each one a story of a voyage survived or a storm endured. He reached into the folds of his cloak and produced a roll of vellum. It was stained with water and age, the edges frayed to white threads.

"The land here is tired," Eirik said. He unrolled the chart on a nearby crate. 

Hallvard leaned in. The ink was faded, some lines nearly vanished into the parchment's grain. It showed coastlines, inlets, and markings that looked like jagged teeth. 

"This is the old way," Eirik whispered. His finger traced a line toward the west. "The men call it Vinland. They say it is a myth of the old ones. I say it is just a place where the trees are still tall enough to build a house that doesn't collapse in a gale."

"It’s far, Eirik," Hallvard said, his voice barely audible over the whistling wind outside. "The sea between here and there... it is not a path for the faint of heart."

Eirik’s eyes hardened. He didn't explain the route. He never did. He simply folded the chart with a sharp snap. "A man can die of hunger in a house with no wood to heat it. Or he can die of salt and spray on a ship with a full belly. I choose the sea."

He left the warehouse then, leaving Hallvard alone with the ghost-scent of resin and the weight of his lie.

***

The longhouse was a cavern of heat and shouting by the time the sun dipped below the horizon. The air was thick with the smell of rendered fat, wet wool, and the sharp tang of fermented ale. 

"We sit here and wait for the frost to turn our blood to ice!" a man named Sigurd bellowed. He slammed a fist onto a communal table, spilling a cup of ale. "The timber is gone! The ships are rotting in the harbor because we have no wood to repair the hulls! We stay, we die."

"We go, and we vanish into the mist," a woman countered, her voice steady but strained. She sat near the hearth, her hands wrapped in furs. "The western sea is a graveyard. My brother didn't come back from the last scouting run. Why should we throw our lives into the foam for a land of stories?"

"Stories don't fill bellies!" Sigurd roared, standing to his full height. 

Hallvard stood in the periphery, leaning against a pillar. He watched the faces of the men and women—the tired professionals of the colony. They were not looking for glory; they were looking for a way to see the next spring. The argument split the room like a crack in an ice floe. On one side, the stayers, whose eyes were clouded with the fear of the unknown. On the other, the goers, whose faces were etched with the desperation of the starving.

Eirik sat in the center of it all, silent as a stone. He didn't join the shouting. He simply watched, his presence a gravity that pulled at the room’s tension. 

The debate raged for an hour, fueled by ale and the mounting pressure of a winter that felt like an approaching predator. Finally, the voices began to taper into low-muttered grievances. The decision had not been made, but the atmosphere had shifted. The choice was no longer *if* they would leave, but *when*.

***

The moon was a sliver of bone in the sky when Hallvard moved toward the docks. 

The *Salt-Bearer* sat at anchor, its clinker-built hull groaning against the tide. It was a knarr—stout and wide-beamed, built for carrying weight rather than speed. It was a beast of burden, meant to haul the wealth of the land across the grey waste of the ocean.

Hallvard moved with practiced efficiency. He didn't need a lantern; he knew the path by heart. 

He reached the hold and began to work. He hauled barrels of salt fish onto the deck, the brine-soaked wood slick against his palms. He moved heavy sacks of pitch, the thick, black substance smelling of ancient forests and fire. Every movement was deliberate, a physical prayer to the necessity of the voyage.

He worked in the shadows, avoiding the gaze of any prowling sentries. He didn't say it aloud—not even to himself. He was signing on for a journey into the unknown. 

His hands bled from the friction of the ropes, and his lungs burned with the intake of cold, salt-heavy air. He thought of Eirik’s chart, and the way the old man’s finger had lingered on the western edge of the world. 

He wasn't looking for a new world. He was looking for a way to keep the people he knew from disappearing into the snow.

As he secured the final barrel of fish, Hallvard paused. He looked out toward the horizon, where the land ended and the grey expanse began. The *Salt-Bearer* felt like a cradle, but it was also a coffin. 

He turned back to the ship’s hull, his fingers tracing a seam in the timber. He would go. He would carry the salt, the fish, and the lies of the ledger into the mist. 

Behind him, the colony was silent, huddled against the coming night, unaware that the first stitch of their departure had already been sewn.

## Chapter 2: The Weight of Rope

The tar smelled of scorched earth and old blood. It clung to Hallvard’s skin, a sticky film that no amount of seawater could truly wash away. He stood in the shadow of the hull, his hands stained black, watching the shipwright hammer a timber into place. Every strike echoed against the ribs of the *knarr*, a hollow thrum that vibrated in Hallvard’s teeth.

"It won't hold," the shipwright grunted. He was a man whose face had been etched into a map of deep lines by forty years of salt air. "The wood is too green. It’ll split before we hit the first gale."

Hallvard didn't argue. He reached into his tunic and pulled out a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth. He unwrapped it to reveal a knife. The hilt was carved from whalebone, smoothed by years of use until it felt like a part of the hand that held it. It had belonged to his father—a man who had lived by the edge of such steel.

"I need the rudder pin," Hallvard said. His voice was flat, stripped of any plea. "The iron one from the forge."

The shipwright took the knife. He weighed it in his hand, his thumb tracing the blade’s edge. He looked at the *knarr*, then back at Hallvard. A long silence stretched between them, filled only by the rhythmic *thwack* of hammers nearby. 

"Your father's blade," the shipwright muttered. "A heavy price for a piece of iron."

"The ship needs to steer," Hallvard replied. "The pin is more important than the steel."

He watched the man move toward the storehouse, the knife disappearing into his belt. Hallvard felt a strange lightness in his tunic where the weight of the blade had lived for months. It was a trade of history for utility. He was stripping away the past to ensure there would be a future at all.

***

The burial happened three days later, in the mud-slicked corner of the launching yard. 

There were no prayers. The wind was too sharp for them, whipping salt spray into their eyes and biting at their cheeks. They dug a shallow trench in the earth that sat between the tide's reach and the settlement’s walls. 

The man inside—Torsten—had died of a lung-fever that had come on as quickly as it had vanished. He hadn't been a hero. He hadn't died in a raid or a storm. He had simply stopped breathing while they were hauling timber, his body collapsing into the slush like a sack of grain.

"He won't be making the voyage," someone muttered. It wasn't an observation; it was a fact as cold as the ground.

Hallvard stood at the edge of the trench. He watched as they lowered the body. There was no ornate casket, only a shroud of coarse wool. They threw a few stones over the mound and kicked the dirt back into place. It was a practical burial—a way to clear the space for those who remained. 

He looked at his hands. The tar was gone, replaced by the grey grime of the yard. He felt the absence of the knife in his pocket more than he expected. Torsten was gone, and the blade was gone, and the ship was still sitting there, waiting to be filled with enough supplies to keep them from starving before they saw land.

***

The *knarr* was finally ready for the crew. It sat in the harbor like a sleeping beast, its clinker-built sides heavy with the weight of salted meat, grain sacks, and wool. 

Inside the hull, the air was thick with the smell of damp wood and the musk of thirty men packed into a space designed for survival, not comfort. Hallvard walked the narrow gangway, his ledger in hand. He was the steward; he was the one who decided who slept where and who ate when.

"I’m not sleeping on the floor with the bilge-rats," a man named Kolur spat. He was a broad-shouldered man with a beard matted with grease. "I've got a wife in the settlement. I won't have my back aching from the damp all night."

Hallvard looked at him, his expression impassive. "The floor is where the dryest wool is kept, Kolur. You sleep where the others sleep. We divide into watches. You’ll have four hours of rest before you’re on the oars again."

"Four hours?" Kolur stepped forward, his chest heaving. "That's barely enough to dream."

"It's what we have," Hallvard said. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to. The authority of the stores was his shield. If they didn't follow the watch, the grain would run out before they saw a single coastline. 

He moved down the line, settling disputes over sleeping sacks and the placement of water barrels. He watched the men—tired professionals who were already beginning to resent the geometry of their new lives. They were being compressed into a singular machine of survival. Every man was a gear; if one jammed, the ship would founder.

Eirik appeared at the stern, his silhouette framed against the grey sky. He didn't look like a king or a conqueror. He looked like a man who knew exactly how much a human life was worth when weighed against a gallon of fresh water.

The crew fell silent as he stepped onto the deck. 

"Listen," Eirik said. His voice carried, low and steady, cutting through the wind. "We go to Vinland not to build an empire in a day. We go to live. And to live, we must have rules for when we touch those shores."

He held up three fingers.

"First: No taking. What is theirs is theirs. We take only what we need to survive, and we take it with permission where possible. We are not raiders today."

A murmur went through the men. Some looked surprised; others looked skeptical. 

"Second," Eirik continued, "No chasing. If they run, let them run. We do not hunt people like wolves hunt deer. We seek a home, not a massacre."

He paused, his eyes scanning the faces of the crew. He saw the hunger in their eyes—the hunger for land, for safety, for something more than the dying timber of Greenland.

"Third: No burning. We do not set fire to what we intend to inhabit. A burned forest gives no shelter. A burned village gives no neighbors."

He lowered his hand. 

"These are the rules. You break them, you answer to me. You follow them, and perhaps we all see the next winter."

Hallvard watched Eirik speak, then looked back at the men. He saw the tension in their jaws, the way they gripped the ropes of the railing. They were being asked to hold back their instincts—to temper their greed with a discipline that felt like another weight to carry. 

He turned back to his ledger. The voyage was no longer a dream of gold and glory. It was a matter of salt, rope, and the terrifying precision of staying alive.

## Chapter 3: Leaving the Known Water

The last of the jagged teeth—the gray rocks that had marked the coast of Greenland—vanished into the mist. It did not happen with a splash or a roar; it simply dissolved, swallowed by a white shroud that tasted of salt and old ice. 

The jokes stopped then. They died as quickly as the land had. On the deck of the *Sif*, the laughter of the previous three nights curdled into a heavy, watchful silence. Men who had spent the evening boasting of their kills or their wives' hair now stared at the horizon with eyes narrowed against the haze. 

Hallvard stood by the mainmast, his hands calloused and stained with the dark residue of tar. He felt the ship’s pulse beneath his boots—the rhythmic groan of the clinker-built hull as it pushed through the swell. It was a steady heartbeat, but today it felt frantic.

"The fog is thickening," someone muttered. 

It was a low voice, barely audible over the slap of water against the hull, but in the silence, it carried like a bell. Hallvard didn't answer. He watched a spray of foam crest over the bow, white and biting. The sea here was different than the coastal waters; it was deeper, colder, and lacked the predictable rhythm of the shallows.

He turned his attention to the water cask. It sat heavy in its cradle, the wood swollen with moisture. His job was to keep them alive, to ensure that every drop was accounted for. He watched a man—a broad-shouldered oarsman named Torsten—reach toward the bucket. 

"Not yet," Hallvard said. His voice was gravelly, used only when necessary. "The morning ration is for after the mid-watch."

Torsten’s face tightened. "My throat feels like it’s been rubbed with sand, Hallvard."

"Then swallow your spit and wait. We are three days from the next known waypoint. If we drink now, we have two less by the time the mist breaks."

The man grumbled but pulled his hand back. That was the way of things now. The luxury of abundance had stayed behind in the fjords. Here, everything was measured: the length of a rope, the weight of a loaf, the amount of water left in the belly of the ship.

By the turn of the watch, the tension shifted from the water to the quarters. 

The *Sif* was cramped. With twelve men and three boys packed into the hold, every inch of dry space was a commodity more precious than silver. A shouting match erupted near the stern—a dispute over a patch of timber that stayed relatively dry even when the spray came over the gunwales.

"It’s my turn for the high berth!" a man named Sigurd barked. He was swaying with the roll of the ship, his face flushed. "I hauled the heaviest lines during the gale."

"You slept there three nights running," another countered, shoving Sigurd's shoulder. "Move back, or I’ll move you."

The argument escalated, voices rising into a jagged cacophony that threatened to wake the sleeping. Hallvard moved. He didn't run; he walked with a deliberate, heavy step that commanded space. He stepped between them, his presence a wall of wool and grim intent.

"Enough," Hallvard said. 

The men fell silent. They looked at him—the steward, the man who knew where their food came from and how much water remained in the cask.

Hallvard looked at Sigurd, then at the man opposite him. He saw the exhaustion in their eyes, the way their shoulders slumped. The ship was a pressure cooker, and they were all looking for an escape from the damp.

"Sigurd, you take the berth," Hallvard commanded. "And you," he pointed to the other man, "you take the floor space by the mast. It’s damp, but it’s yours."

"What about your spot?" Sigurd asked, his voice still trembling with leftover adrenaline.

Hallvard looked at his own pallet—a meager arrangement of furs and wool that was currently soaked through from a leak in the overhead seam. He felt the cold seep into his bones. 

"I'll take the floor," Hallvard said. "Move."

They moved. The silence returned, heavier than before. Hallvard sat down on the hard timber, the dampness soaking into his tunic. It was a small sacrifice of comfort, but he knew that if one man felt cheated, the whole crew would begin to fray. A frayed crew was a dead ship.

He sat for a moment in the gloom before a shadow moved near him. 

It was Ketil, the youngest of the boys. He was barely sixteen, with skin that hadn't yet lost its childhood roundness and hands that still shook when they grew cold. The boy was huddled against the bulkhead, trying to make himself small.

"Hallvard?" he whispered.

"Speak."

"The men... they talk about what happens if we fall over the side. If the sea takes us." Ketil’s voice broke on the last word. He looked up at Hallvard, his eyes wide and shimmering with a terror that hadn't been suppressed by the routine of the voyage. "I can’t swim, Hallvard. I tried in the river back home, but I sink like a stone. I don't know how to find the top."

The admission hung in the air, thick and suffocating. In the Norse world, a man who could not swim was a liability—a weight that might pull others down. If the captain knew, Ketil might be relegated to the deepest part of the hold, or worse, he might be seen as unfit for the voyage entirely.

Hallvard looked at the boy. He saw the fear, but he also saw the honesty. It was a heavy thing to carry—a secret that could get a boy cast out or left behind. 

"Keep your head down," Hallvard said softly. He didn't offer a platitude; there were no easy words for the sea. "Stay near the center of the ship. Don't go near the gunwales when the swells are high."

Ketil exhaled, a long, shuddering breath. "You won't tell them?"

"I told you nothing," Hallvard lied. It was a lie that felt like lead in his mouth. 

He looked away from the boy. He would keep this secret. Ketil would be his responsibility now—a debt of protection. If the boy fell, it would be on Hallvard’s head. He watched the boy retreat into the shadows of the hold, and for the first time in many years, Hallvard felt a pang of something that wasn't duty. It was pity.

The next morning brought a change in the air. The fog didn't lift, but it thinned, turning from a wall of white to a translucent veil of gray. 

Eirik stood at the bow, his face etched with lines of concentration. He was a man of few words, often dismissed by the others as a dreamer because he spent too much time watching the horizon and not enough time hauling rope. But today, he moved with a sudden, sharp purpose.

He squinted into the gray. "Birds," he said.

The crew gathered, their curiosity overriding their exhaustion. 

"What kind?" someone asked.

Eirik didn't answer immediately. He watched a small cluster of shapes moving against the wind. They were erratic, dipping and rising in the haze. He waited until they disappeared before turning back to the men.

"They are heading south-southwest," Eirik said. His voice was flat, devoid of the pride that usually accompanied such claims. 

He took a hand-line—a length of rope with a lead weight—and cast it into the water. He let it drop, watching the tension in the line. He didn't look at the depth; he looked at the way the birds behaved as they moved toward the land that wasn't there yet.

"How do you know?" Sigurd asked, leaning forward. "You can barely see them."

Eirik looked at him. His eyes were like flint. "The wind carries their scent. The water tells me where they rest."

"Is it a bearing?" another man asked. "Give us a number."

Eirik tightened his grip on the line. He didn't give a number. He didn't give a direction beyond the vague movement of the birds. He simply stood there, a sentinel in the mist, refusing to explain the mechanics of his intuition. 

He knew something they did not—that the land was close enough for the birds to find it, but far enough that the sea still held its teeth.

Hallvard watched Eirik from the stern. He saw the way the man’s hands trembled slightly as he pulled the line back up. It wasn't fear; it was the strain of holding a secret knowledge against the weight of the unknown. 

The ship groaned, turning its nose toward the direction Eirik had indicated. The fog remained, but for the first time in six nights, there was a flicker of something other than dread in the men’s eyes. It wasn't hope—hope was for those on land. It was merely the grim determination of those who still had a path to follow.

Hallvard turned back to his cask. He gripped the handle, feeling the cold moisture of the wood. They were moving forward. Whether they were moving toward life or the bottom of the sea remained to be seen.

## Chapter 4: Landfall

The *Salt-Bearer* did not glide into the bay so much as she surrendered to it. 

The hull groaned, a deep, shuddering sound of oak against stone, and then the keel bit into the grey sand of the shallows. Hallvard stood on the waist, his hands gripping the salt-crusted railing until his knuckles turned the color of the foam. The ship lurched sideways as the tide pushed against her broad beam, and for a moment, she sat heavy and awkward in the surf, trapped between the hungry sea and the indifferent land.

"She’s grounded! We’re stuck!" Bjorn shouted from the bow, his voice cracking with a mixture of terror and relief. He was doubled over, one hand on a stay-rope, the other bracing against a timber. "The tide is turning against us! If we don't get her off, she’ll be high and dry by noon!"

"She isn't stuck yet," Hallvard said, though his own stomach tightened. He stepped forward, his boots slick with seawater. "Look at the water. It’s still moving. We haul the line and we pivot. If we stay here too long, she’ll be a sitting duck for every scavenger bird in the bay."

"Pivot? With what strength?" Another man, a younger lad whose face was smeared with soot and brine, gestured to the crew. They were swaying, their movements sluggish, eyes sunken from six nights of watching the horizon for a ghost that refused to appear. "They can barely stand, let alone pull her."

Hallvard looked at his men. He saw the way their shoulders slumped, the way they leaned into one another just to stay upright. They were tired—not just the tiredness of labor, but the hollow ache of bodies running out of fuel. 

"We don't need to move her yet," Hallvard said, his voice carrying a flat, pragmatic authority that cut through Bjorn’s panic. "We need to unload. We take the heaviest crates first. We get weight off her belly, and she’ll sit higher. If we can’t move her by the next turn of the tide, then we pray to the All-Father for a heavy swell."

The work began with the frantic energy of the desperate. They threw hempen lines toward the shore, anchoring them to submerged rocks and heavy boulders. The *Salt-Bearer* gave another groan as they began to heave the primary stores onto the beach. 

Hallvard watched the crates hit the sand. It was a grim inventory—barrels of salted meat, sacks of grain that were dangerously light, and the remaining jars of oil. Each one represented a day of life bought or lost. He felt the weight of the stewardship in his chest; every pound of salt-beef moved was a calculation of survival.

By midday, the beach was a graveyard of supplies. The sun was a pale, heatless coin hanging in a grey sky, casting long shadows over the grass and rock. 

"Timber," Eirik commanded. He stood near the water’s edge, his cloak wrapped tight against the wind that swept in from the open sea. "We need to clear a space for the landing. We can't have the tide washing our stores back into the deep."

The men took up axes and crosscut saws. The wood here was thick—massive, ancient trunks of some unknown tree, their bark scarred by weather but their hearts solid. They began to fell them, the rhythmic *thwack-crunch* of steel hitting wood echoing off the surrounding cliffs. 

Hallvard joined them, his own axe feeling heavy in his hands. He swung until his palms blistered, the skin weeping into the raw wood. The irony of the land was not lost on him; they had spent weeks starving for a single pallet of grain, and now they were surrounded by more timber than ten ships could carry. It was an abundance that felt like a burden. There was too much to move, too much to stack, too much to manage.

"Look at this," Bjorn muttered, pausing to wipe sweat from his brow despite the chill. He pointed to a pile of felled trunks. "We could build a hall here. A proper longhouse."

Hallvard didn't answer. He was looking further up the beach, where the grass grew taller and the grey rocks gave way to a sheltered hollow. 

He pushed away from the main work area, his breath coming in short, ragged bursts. The air here smelled different—less of salt and more of damp earth and old smoke. He rounded a large outcropping of rock and stopped.

There, nestled in a fold of the land, was a fire pit. 

It wasn't a haphazard heap of stones. It was lined with heavy, flat rocks, arranged with a precision that spoke of intent. The center was blackened, the soot still clinging to the underside of the stones despite the wind. Beside it lay the remains of a wooden frame—the skeletal ribs of a skin tent. 

Hallvard knelt by the pit. He touched a stone; it was cold, but he could see where the earth had been scorched. The wood of the frame wasn't rotted away by years of rain or splintered by winter gales. It looked as though it had been dismantled, or perhaps simply left behind only a few seasons ago.

A chill that had nothing to do with the wind crawled up his spine. 

They were not the first here. The land was already claimed, even if the claimants were gone for the moment. He looked at the frame again. It wasn't Norse construction—the joinery was different, the curves of the wood suggested a different hand. 

"Hallvard!" Eirik’s voice drifted from the beach. "The men are complaining about the weight. We need to move the primary stores inland."

Hallvard stood, his heart hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird. He looked back at the fire pit one last time before turning toward the captain. 

"They're right," Hallvard called back, his voice steady despite the sudden tightness in his throat. "The tide will take everything we’ve put down if we leave it on the open beach."

Eirik approached him, his face a mask of grim determination. He looked at the piles of timber, then toward the inland path they had been eyeing. 

"Move the camp," Eirik ordered. "We don't stay on the shore. We move into the trees. I want the stores shielded from the wind and hidden from the sight of the water."

The crew moved with a renewed sense of urgency, though their faces remained etched with exhaustion. They hauled the crates inland, dragging them over the grass and past the grey rocks toward a stretch of land they began to call 'the Long Shore.' 

As they worked, Hallvard stayed at the rear, watching the beach they were leaving behind. He thought of the fire pit and the frame. It was a quiet thing, a small piece of evidence in a vast, unknown world. But it changed the shape of the voyage. They were no longer just survivors of a storm; they were intruders in someone else's home.

The sun dipped lower, casting the long shadows of the trees across the grass. The men began to strike flint, trying to coax life into small fires for warmth. Hallvard sat on an overturned crate, his blistered hands resting in his lap. 

He looked out toward the bay. The *Salt-Bearer* sat low in the sand, a battered hull against the darkening horizon. For the first time in weeks, they were no longer at sea. But as the first stars began to prick through the grey veil of the evening, Hallvard felt a new kind of tension—a weight heavier than any crate of timber. 

The land was here. And the land had eyes.

## Chapter 5: Salt-Bearer

The ledger was a lie. 

Hallvard sat on a crate of damp wool, the charcoal stick in his hand hovering over a page of cramped Norse script. On paper, the numbers were elegant. They suggested a surplus of salt-beef and enough grain to see the crew through the first frost. Reality, however, possessed a much more pungent odor.

He wiped a bead of sweat from his brow with a sleeve that had long since lost its ability to stay dry. Before him stood three barrels, their staves swollen and weeping a dark, viscous liquid. He had spent the morning prying them open with a heavy iron lever, expecting the crisp, briny scent of preserved meat. Instead, he found the sour stench of rot—the slow, wet death of protein surrendered to moisture.

"The seal on the third one was compromised," Hallvard said, his voice flat. He didn't look up at Eirik, who stood behind him, his shadow stretching long across the dirt floor of the makeshift storehouse. "The salt has turned to grey sludge. The meat is... unusable."

Eirik leaned in, his face a map of deep-set lines and wind-burned skin. He didn't need to see the contents to understand the implication. In the math of the North, rot was a thief that stole days from their lives. 

"How many?" Eirik asked.

"Enough to mean we must tighten the portions," Hallvard replied. He finally looked up. His eyes were tired, reflecting the grey sky outside. "We cannot afford to be generous with the grain anymore. If we keep eating as we did last week, we will be hollowed out before the first snow sticks."

The men moved around them in a haze of weary industry. Outside, the *knarr* sat heavy in the mud of the Long Shore, its hull groaning against the weight of the timber they were stripping from the nearby woods. The forest here was an oppressive wall of green and brown, the trees standing so thick that light struggled to reach the floor. It was a prize of wood, but it was also a cage of branches.

By mid-afternoon, the first meal of the day was served. 

It was a grim affair. They sat on overturned buckets and logs, the silence broken only by the rhythmic chewing of tough meat and the occasional crackle of the fire. Hallvard watched them—tired professionals who had traded their homes for a horizon that kept moving. He saw the way they looked at each other over the steam of the pot, measuring the meager portions. 

A young man near the edge of the circle, his hands raw from hauling timber, let out a low groan as he pushed a piece of gristle around his bowl.

"Eat it," Hallvard said. It wasn't a suggestion; it was an order from the man who held the ledger. "The math doesn't care if it tastes like leather."

There was no argument. They were too tired to argue. The winter math had shifted, and the crew was beginning to feel the weight of the deficit in their bellies.

On the third morning, the world turned grey with mist before the sun could claim it. 

Hallvard was outside, overseeing the distribution of water when he felt a shift in the atmosphere. It wasn't a sound—not yet—but a change in the way the air felt against his skin. He looked toward the far shore, across a stretch of water that had remained still as glass all night.

A line of figures appeared.

They did not emerge from the trees like ghosts; they moved with a deliberate, practiced economy. They were tall, draped in furs and skins that blended into the muted tones of the shoreline. They stood in a row, positioned against the rise of the land, watching.

The camp went silent. The sound of axes hitting wood ceased. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

For a long minute, no one moved. Hallvard felt his own heart hammer against his ribs—a frantic bird in a cage. They were being watched by eyes that were not his own, by people who lived in this land while he was merely visiting it. 

"Should we...?" a man started to ask, his hand hovering near the hilt of a seax. "Wave them? Or call out?"

The crew looked at Eirik. The captain stood near the bow of the *knarr*, his hands gripped tight on a railing. His face was unreadable, a mask of pragmatic caution. He saw the figures as clearly as Hallvard did—competent, watchful, and entirely indifferent to their presence. They were not posing; they were observing a new variable in their environment.

"No," Eirik said. The word was low, like a stone dropping into deep water. "We do not wave. We do not call."

He turned his gaze back to his men, his voice regaining its command. 

"They are here. They have seen us. Let them see that we are busy with our work. Do not offer them a gesture they might misinterpret as an invitation or a challenge."

The figures remained for another heartbeat, then dissolved back into the treeline with a fluidity that suggested they knew every root and rock of the shore. They were gone before anyone could decide if they were friends or rivals.

Eirik walked toward Hallvard, his boots crunching on the frost-touched dirt. "They will be back," he said. It wasn't a fear; it was an observation of geography. "People who live here do not move twice for no reason."

"What are your orders?" Hallvard asked, his voice steady despite the chill in his marrow.

Eirik looked at the woods, then at the *knarr*, and finally at the men who were beginning to huddle together in the growing cold. 

"We do not pursue. We do not approach," Eirik commanded. "But we will not be caught sleeping. Double the watch. I want a fire kept burning on the western ridge tonight—not a large one, just enough to see the shadows move. If they come to the water's edge, we know."

Hallvard nodded. He turned back to his ledger, but he didn't open it. The numbers were no longer the most important thing in the camp. The figures on the shore had changed the math entirely.

## Chapter 6: Two Fires

The cold did not have a voice, but it had a weight. It sat upon Ketil’s shoulders like a yoke of frozen iron, pressing him down into the white crust of the snow. 

He gripped the handles of the wood-sledge, his fingers numbing inside the cracked leather wraps Eirik had provided. They were failing. The leather was damp with melted frost, and the skin beneath it was turning a dull, painful purple. He didn't mind the pain as much as the silence of it; when the nerves went dead, the world felt thinner, as if he might slip through the atmosphere and vanish into the grey sky.

"Dry wood only," Eirik’s voice echoed in his mind. "If it hisses, it’s useless for the hearth. We need the heartwood, not the brush."

Ketil moved with a slow, rhythmic heave. The sledge groaned against the packed snow, its runners biting into the earth. He was a long way from the main camp now, far enough that the low hum of the crew's voices had been swallowed by the wind. Here, there was only the sound of his own breathing—short, sharp puffs of steam—and the rhythmic *crunch-slide* of the sledge.

He found a stand of stunted birch and alder. The branches were heavy with ice, hanging like glass ornaments. He swung his axe, the blade biting into the wood with a dull thud. He worked slowly. To swing fast was to tire; to tire was to freeze. He took only what he could carry back, selecting the thickest limbs, stripping them of their icy coating until they were clean and pale.

He was halfway back when the wind shifted. 

It didn't bring a new scent—the air was too sterile for that—but it brought a change in the light. A smudge of orange appeared against the horizon, nestled in the lee of a granite outcropping. It wasn't the flickering glow of their own fires; it was steady, concentrated.

Ketil stopped. He did not move for a long time, his breath hitching in his chest. He watched the smoke. It rose in a straight, thin line, indicating a sheltered spot. 

He should have turned back. The rules were simple: stay with the group when the shadows grew long. But the wood was heavy, and his legs were already beginning to cramp. If he didn't move now, he would be trapped in the drift until the sun vanished completely.

He moved toward the orange light. He kept his weight low, his hands white-knuckled on the sledge handles. 

The outcropping was a jagged tooth of stone. Tucked into its shadow, three figures sat around a fire. They were not Norse. They wore furs that were different from the sheepskin and seal-hide of the crew—longer hairs, darker hues, stitched with sinew in patterns that looked like braided grass.

Ketil stopped twenty paces away. 

The strangers did not jump. They did not reach for weapons. One of them, a man whose face was etched with lines as deep as the riverbeds, looked up from a pot suspended over the flames. He held a wooden paddle. Beside him sat two others—a woman with hair braided into tight coils and a younger man who watched Ketil with an intensity that made the boy’s stomach turn.

They were boiling something. A thick, greyish mash that released a heavy, earthy steam. It smelled of roots and something fermented.

Ketil felt the weight of his own presence. He was a speck of dust in their world. He looked at the sledge, then at the fire. His throat felt like it had been scraped with sand. 

He reached into the small pouch at his belt. It contained two pieces of flatbread—the hard, dense loaves they had been rationing since the cows died. They were his portion for the next two days. In the current climate of the camp, bread was more than food; it was survival.

He stepped forward. He didn't speak. There were no words to bridge the gap. 

The man with the paddle watched him. He did not move.

Ketil reached out and held one piece of bread toward the fire. It was a gesture of peace, or perhaps just an admission of hunger. He let his hand tremble slightly.

The woman moved first. She reached into a basket at her side and pulled out a bundle wrapped in a large, dried leaf. She did not stand. She simply extended her arm.

Ketil hesitated, then took it. 

He retreated a few paces, keeping the sledge between him and the fire. He sat on the runners, his muscles screaming in protest, and unwrapped the leaf. Inside were three fish—small, oily things, steamed until the flesh was flaking off the bone. They were hot enough to sting his fingers. 

He took a bite of the bread. It was dry, tasting of toasted grain and dust. He chewed slowly, letting the moisture from the fish soften the crumb. 

The strangers watched him eat. They did not smile, but they did not look hostile. They sat in their own circle, eating their mash, watching him with a clinical curiosity. It was a transaction of calories—a momentary truce signed in salt and grain.

For ten minutes, there was only the sound of chewing and the crackle of the fire. No one spoke. The silence was heavy, but not sharp. It was the silence of two different kinds of tired meeting in the same patch of cold earth. 

Ketil finished the fish. He felt a strange warmth blooming in his chest—not just from the food, but from the realization that these people were like him. They were here, in the middle of the winter, trying to keep their insides warm while the world tried to turn them into ice.

He stood up, his joints popping like dry twigs. He placed the remaining piece of bread on a flat stone near the fire—a gift, or perhaps a payment. 

He turned and began the long trek back.

The return was harder. The shadows had stretched into long, distorted fingers, and the wind had regained its teeth. Ketil pushed the sledge, his lungs burning with every inhalation of freezing air. He didn't look back. He didn't need to know what they did with the bread or what happened to the three fish he had left behind.

When he finally crested the rise toward the camp, the lanterns were already lit. The atmosphere was thick with the smell of wet wool and the sharp, acidic tang of sickness. 

He stumbled into the clearing, the sledge groaning to a halt. 

Eirik was standing near the main fire, his face cast in orange light. He looked older than he had that morning—his beard was frosted white, and his eyes were rimmed with red. Two men were huddled nearby, shivering under heavy furs; they were the ones the fever had claimed first.

"You’re late," Eirik said. His voice was a low rasp. 

Ketil tried to speak, but his throat was too dry. He managed only a small, jagged nod.

"The wood?" Eirik asked, stepping forward. He looked at the sledge, then at Ketil’s face—at the smudge of soot on his cheek and the way he seemed to be staring into some distant space. "You found the wood."

Ketil nodded again. He wanted to tell him about the fire. He wanted to tell him about the three people who had shared a meal with him in the middle of nowhere. He wanted to tell him that they weren't monsters, and they weren't ghosts; they were just hungry.

But as he looked at Eirik’s face—the face of a man watching his crew wither away—he realized the words would be too complicated. They would be misinterpreted. 

"Did you see anyone?" Eirik asked, his voice dropping an octave. "The scouts said there were watches on the far shore. I need to know if they are moving inland."

Ketil opened his mouth. The truth was a heavy thing in his throat. *I met them. We ate together.* 

He looked at Eirik’s hands—rough, scarred, and shaking slightly from the cold. He saw the fear there, hidden behind the pragmatism of leadership. If he told the truth, it might bring a different kind of tension to the camp. If he lied, he was betraying the only thing he had left: his own eyes.

He stayed silent for a heartbeat too long. 

Eirik sighed, a sound that seemed to deflate his chest. "You're shaking, boy. You saw something that bothered you." He gestured toward a crate near the fire where a stick of charcoal lay. "Sit. You can’t tell me what happened in the dark, and I don’t have the strength to listen to a story. Draw it for me. Show me what you saw."

Ketil sat. His hands were so cold they felt like stones. He took the charcoal. 

He didn't draw the wood. He didn't draw the snow. 

He drew a circle—the fire. He drew three figures, their heads bowed, their bodies wrapped in heavy skins. He drew the sledge, and he drew himself, a small shape huddled against the weight of the world.

He held the charcoal-stained paper up. Eirik took it, his eyes scanning the lines. For a moment, the only sound was the wind howling through the gaps in the timber walls.

Eirik looked at the drawing, then at Ketil. He didn't look angry. He looked tired. 

"They have fire," Eirik whispered, more to himself than to the boy. "They have fire and they are still here."

He handed the paper back to Ketil. It was a record now. A piece of evidence. A ghost of a meeting that had happened in the grey between the trees. 

Ketil watched the charcoal smudge on his fingers, feeling the first true ache of the coming night. The bread was gone, and the fish was in his belly, but the drawing remained—a bridge made of soot and ash.

## Chapter 7: The Long Dark

The snow did not fall so much as it occupied the air. It was a heavy, damp weight that turned the world into a study of grey on white. Hallvard stood by the timber wall of the longhouse, his breath blooming in front of him like a ghost’s sigh. He watched the flakes settle on the salt-crusted hull of the knarr, burying the carvings of the prow until only the highest ridges remained visible.

He reached into his tunic and pulled out a piece of charcoal and a scrap of vellum. His fingers were stiff, the joints aching with a dull, rhythmic throb that matched the pulsing wind outside. He looked at the numbers he had been scratching out in his head for three days—the remaining weight of the grain, the dwindling barrels of salted meat, the count of able-bodied men versus the number of mouths to feed.

He did not write them down. 

To put the numbers on paper was to make them true. If he wrote that they had forty days of food left for the crew alone, it became a fact that could be shouted in the mess hall. If he kept it in his mind, it remained a shadow, something he could manage by stretching the portions and hiding the thinning broth. He folded the vellum and tucked it back into his sleeve. A steward’s job was to provide; a man's job was to survive. Sometimes those two duties collided until they bruised one another.

A low groan came from the center of the camp. 

Hallvard turned. In the mud and slush, the last cow lay on its side. It was a large beast, its ribs protruding like the hull-frames of a wrecked ship. It had been shivering for hours, its breath coming in ragged, wet bursts. Now, it was still. The steam stopped rising from its nostrils. 

The men gathered around it—not with reverence, but with the grim, heavy movements of those who knew that the death of a cow was the death of a safety net. They were tired men, their faces lined by salt-spray and the relentless wind. One of them, a man named Orm whose beard was matted with frost, kicked at the carcass.

"It’s gone," Orm muttered. His voice was flat, stripped of any mourning. "The fat is gone too."

"We will butcher it tonight," Hallvard said. He didn't want to say *it won't be enough.* 

A murmur went through the group—a low, curdled sound of resentment. They were hungry. The men knew the cow was the last of the livestock that could provide tallow and meat for the deep winter. When it died of the cold, the remaining stores became a finite pool, and every man felt his own portion shrinking in his mind.

"The men are talking, Hallvard," a voice said from the doorway.

Eirik stood there, his cloak heavy with moisture. He looked older than he had when they first weighed anchor. The weight of the voyage was etched into the corners of his eyes. 

"They are talking because they are cold and their bellies are empty, Eirik," Hallvard replied. "It is the natural state of a man in a blizzard."

"They are talking about the strangers," Eirik said. He stepped into the warmth of the longhouse, though he didn't sit. "And they are right to be wary. The scouts say there are more of them inland. A group. They move like people who know these woods."

Hallvard looked at the door-stone—a heavy, flat rock placed at the threshold of the camp’s main enclosure. It was a symbol of their boundary, the point where the law of the ship met the law of the land. 

"They are coming again," Hallvard said. "The ones from the fire. They will be looking for warmth."

"They will find it," Eirik said.

Hallvard felt a spark of irritation, sharp as a flint-strike. "With what? We have lost the cow. The grain is measured in handfuls now. If we feed them as we did the first time, we are shortening our own lives by weeks."

Eirik’s expression didn't change, but his posture stiffened. "We are guests of this land, Hallvard. And they are guests of our fire. The law of hospitality is not a calculation of grain weight. It is what separates us from the wolves."

"The wolves don't have to worry about starving in three months," Hallvard countered. He stepped toward his ledger, his voice dropping into a low, pragmatic hiss. "I see the numbers. I see how many days we have left if we share everything equally. It is not enough for both of us to survive."

Eirik looked at him, and for a moment, the steward saw the captain—not the man who shared bread, but the man who commanded a ship. "Then let the math be your burden, Hallvard. Not mine. I will keep the door open."

The wind shrieked then, a high-pitched wail that rattled the timber walls. It was the sound of the bay closing, the final seal of winter. 

In the middle of the night, they came.

They did not arrive with a shout or a charge. They appeared as shadows against the white expanse, moving with a fluidity that suggested they were part of the landscape itself. There were more than three this time—at least seven, some carrying bundles wrapped in furs, others moving with the steady, rhythmic gait of those who had walked many miles through deep snow.

They stopped at the edge of the camp, where the light of the central fire struggled against the encroaching dark. They did not approach the fire immediately. They stood there, their faces obscured by furs, watching the Norsemen. 

Hallvard stood by the door-stone, his hand resting on a heavy iron-headed tool. He felt the tension in the men around him—the way they gripped their axes, the way their eyes darted toward the shadows. 

"They are many," Orm whispered from behind Hallvard. "Should we let them in?"

Hallvard looked at Eirik. The captain was standing near the fire, his face illuminated by the dancing orange light. He looked exhausted, but his chin was set.

"The door is open," Eirik said softly.

One of the strangers stepped forward. He was tall, his hair braided with strips of hide. He held out a hand—not in a gesture of surrender, but in a simple offering. In his palm lay a small bundle of dried roots and a piece of greyish meat. 

It was a peace offering. A recognition of shared hardship.

Hallvard watched as Eirik took the gift. The captain nodded once, a gesture of acceptance that seemed to ripple through the gathered Norsemen. 

"Bring them in," Eirik commanded. "But keep them to the outer tables. We share the fire, but we do not share the beds tonight."

The strangers entered. They moved with a quiet competence, their movements economical and purposeful. They did not speak; they did not need to. The silence between the two groups was heavy, filled with the unspoken weight of the snow outside and the desperate necessity of the heat inside.

Hallvard walked to the large communal pot. He took a ladle and began to distribute the broth. It was thin, mostly water and a few scraps of salted meat, but it was hot. 

He watched the strangers eat. They ate with a focused intensity, their eyes never leaving the fire. They were not there for gold or glory; they were there for calories. They were survivalists, just as his men were. There was a grim dignity in their faces—a shared recognition of the "Long Dark."

As the night deepened, the atmosphere in the longhouse shifted from wary tension to a weary, communal exhaustion. The strangers sat on the periphery, their presence a constant reminder of the world outside the camp's walls. 

Hallvard moved among them, his face a mask of professional neutrality. He handed out bowls, he monitored the fire, and he watched the way the men and the strangers avoided each other’s eyes. It was a fragile truce held together by the shared miracle of warmth.

By the time the fire had burned down to glowing embers, the shivering had subsided into a dull ache. The strangers were beginning to retreat toward the shadows of the camp's edge, their task of survival for the night complete. 

Except for two.

Two of them remained seated near the door-stone. They were older, their faces etched with the deep furrows of many winters. They did not move as the others drifted toward the sleeping mats. They sat in the fading light, watching the Norsemen sleep.

Hallvard stood by the door-stone, his back aching, his head spinning with the math he refused to write down. He looked at the two remaining strangers, then at Eirik, who was leaning against a timber post, watching the same shadows.

"They aren't leaving," Hallvard whispered.

Eirik didn't look at him. "I know."

Hallvard took a breath, the cold air of the night creeping in through the cracks in the logs. He thought of the grain, the dead cow, and the two men who would now be his responsibility. 

He reached into his tunic, felt the vellum, and for the first time in weeks, he closed his eyes and let the numbers settle into a different kind of truth. The math had changed. They were no longer just surviving; they were hosting. And in the Long Dark, that was a much heavier burden to bear.

## Chapter 8: What the Guests Knew

The wind did not merely blow; it sought out the seams of the world, prying into the gaps between the timber walls and the heavy hides draped over the doors. It carried the scent of old snow and the sharp, metallic tang of deep frost.

Hallvard sat on a low stool, his fingers aching in a way that felt permanent. He was a steward by trade, a man whose life was measured in sacks of grain, barrels of salt pork, and the steady tally of what remained versus what was needed to see another week of winter. In the dim light of the central fire, the faces of the crew were etched in grey—shadows of men who had seen their stores dwindle and their bodies grow heavy with the lethargy of the cold.

Two of the strangers remained. They had stayed when the others had retreated into the inner sanctum of the longhouse or huddled in their furs. They sat across from Hallvard, their presence a quiet weight in the room. They did not speak much Norse, and Hallvard’s own tongue was clumsy when trying to shape the sounds they made, but work required no common vocabulary.

Between them lay a heap of tangled netting—the heavy hempen mesh used for the larger catches.

Hallvard took a length of twine, his hands moving with the rhythmic precision of a man who had spent twenty years on deck. *Loop. Pull. Knot.* Beside him, one of the strangers did the same. The man was older, his face a map of deep creases, his hair the color of bleached bone. He worked with a steady hand that didn't tremble, even as the fire crackled and threw sparks against the rafters.

"The tide," the stranger said suddenly. His voice was low, a gravelly rasp that seemed to come from deep in his chest. He pointed toward the heavy door, where the wind howled. "It moves before the moon is full."

Hallvard paused, his needle hovering over a tear in the mesh. He looked at the man. To the Norsemen, the tide was a thing of fate—a cycle to be observed and endured. They knew when it turned, but they did not know *why* or how to read its pulse beyond what the sea gave them.

The stranger reached into a pouch at his belt and pulled out a strip of cured leather. On it were markings—not runes, nor letters, but a series of precise notches and shaded lines. He pointed to a section and then toward the bay outside. 

"We know the water’s path," the man said in his broken tongue. "The fish come when the water pushes into the rocks. We do not hunt where they are not."

Hallvard looked at the leather, then back at the net. He realized with a jolt of clarity that these men weren't just surviving; they were calculating. They had tables for the sea’s movements. While the Norsemen relied on the strength of their arms and the luck of their eyes, these people lived by a measured understanding of the water's rhythm. 

He looked at his own hands—calloused, scarred, but ultimately guessing at the sea's moods. He felt a strange prickle of humility. They were guests in his camp, yet they possessed a map of the invisible.

A few hours passed in the shared silence of labor. The fire burned down to glowing embers, and the air grew thick with the smell of wet wool and woodsmoke. 

"The landing," the stranger said again, his eyes fixed on the flickering orange light. He gestured toward the direction of the shore where they had first struck their ships. "The fire-pit you found. It is not a grave for old fires."

Hallvard frowned. He remembered the site—a circle of stones, blackened and smoothed by years of use. They had assumed it was a relic of some long-dead tribe, a place where someone had once sat to keep the night at bay. 

"It is a signal," the man said. He leaned forward, his face illuminated by the dying fire. "When the inland people move, they look for the spark. It tells them where the water meets the land. It tells them who stays."

The realization hit Hallvard like a physical blow. The site wasn't a piece of history; it was a piece of infrastructure. It meant there were others—not just these two men, but a network of people moving through the interior, watching the coast, communicating across distances that stretched beyond the sight of the horizon.

The door creaked open, admitting a blast of air so cold it made the fire hiss. Eirik stood in the entrance, his heavy cloak dusted with white. He looked older than he had a week ago, the weight of command carving deeper lines into his brow. 

He didn't come to join them at the fire. He walked straight to the table where the supplies were kept and pulled out a piece of parchment—the chart they had used to navigate the coastline.

"The others are moving," Eirik said, his voice barely above a whisper. "The scouts who went inland... they returned with something."

He held up a small bundle of cord. It was made of some fibrous plant material, not hemp or wool. It was tied with a series of complex knots—loops and hitches that defied Hallvard’s understanding of maritime rope-work. 

"It came from the interior," Eirik said. "The strangers say it is a message."

Hallvard looked at the knots. They were beautiful in their intricacy, yet they held no meaning for him. He felt like a man looking at a locked door without a key. 

"Who sent it?" Hallvard asked.

Eirik sighed, a long, weary sound. He laid the chart flat on the table. "The strangers say there is a third group. Further inland. They are not like the ones we saw near the river. They do not trade. They do not wait."

He pointed to the margin of the chart—the vast, empty space where the ink stopped and the parchment remained white. 

"I told you this voyage was limited," Eirik said, his voice heavy with a sudden, grim honesty. "The chart ends here because we do not know what lies beyond the next ridge. We have sailed into a room with no windows, Hallvard. These knots... they are a warning that someone else is in the room with us."

Hallvard looked at the knotted cord again. He thought of the stranger’s tide tables and the signal fire. The world was much larger than their ship, and much more crowded than they had dared to imagine. 

The wind screamed against the walls, a lonely, mourning sound. Outside, the winter held its breath, waiting for the next move. Hallvard gripped his needle until his knuckles turned white, the silence of the room heavier than the cold.

## Chapter 9: The Argument in the Longhouse

The smoke in the longhouse hung low, a grey shroud that tasted of peat and old grease. It clung to the rafters and stung the eyes of those who sat closest to the central fire. Outside, the wind had begun to lose its bite, shifting from a sharp blade to a dull ache, but inside, the air was thick with a different kind of heat—the friction of voices raised against the looming thaw.

Hallvard sat on a bench of hewn oak, his hands resting on the ledger. The leather cover was cracked and stained with salt spray, its edges softened by years of use. He felt the weight of it, not just in his palms but in his chest. It was the book of truth. In a land where the horizon kept shifting and the trees grew tall enough to swallow the sun, the ledger was the only thing that stayed still.

“The timber is the finest we have seen since the first landing,” Bjorn said, his voice gravelly from months of shouting over the wind. He stood near the fire, his face smeared with soot. He gestured toward the heavy logs stacked against the wall—wood so dense it rang like iron when struck. “We could build a permanent hall here. We could clear the ridge and plant-side. Why go back to the old ships? Why risk the sea when the land is opening up for us?”

A murmur of agreement rippled through the men. They were tired. Their wool tunics were heavy with the grime of winter, and their joints ached from the constant labor of survival. To them, the idea of staying—of carving a life into this soil—was a seductive promise of rest.

Hallvard didn't look up at Bjorn. He looked at the columns of figures he had spent months meticulously updating. 

“The timber is good,” Hallvard said. His voice was quiet, but it carried. “It is the best wood in the world. It will hold a roof against a gale.”

He opened the ledger to the final page of the winter tally. He cleared his throat, the sound sharp in the sudden silence of the room. 

“We have three barrels of salted cod remaining,” he began, reading plainly. “The grain stores are at half-measure, and the tallow is low enough that we cannot grease the oars for more than a month’s voyage without thinning it with fat. We have enough to see the spring through, yes. But if we stay to build, if we stay to farm this ridge, we will be eating boiled leather by the time the first frost returns.”

He turned the page. The numbers were cold and unyielding. They did not care about the quality of the timber or the beauty of the valley. 

“To stay is to gamble on a harvest we haven't planted yet,” Hallvard continued, his eyes scanning the ink. “To leave is to carry what we have and find the next shore. We are a ship’s crew, not a village. If we remain here, we are waiting for the land to provide. If we sail, we take what we have with us.”

The room went still. The fire popped, sending a spray of sparks against the soot-stained walls. Hallvard felt Eirik’s gaze on him from across the flames. Eirik sat in the shadows, his face unreadable, the weight of command etched into the deep lines around his mouth. 

In the corner, the strangers sat together. They were wrapped in furs that smelled of woodsmoke and earth. They had watched the argument with a steady, piercing intensity. They did not speak as the Norse men spoke; they moved with a deliberate economy of motion. When Hallvard finished reading the last line of the ledger, he looked at them.

A man among them—one who had spent many nights watching the stars with the crew—leaned forward. He looked at the remaining supplies, then at the heavy timber, and finally at the men who were deciding their fate. 

He spoke a single word in his own tongue. It was a sound like stones grinding together.

The Norse men looked at one another. They didn't know what it meant. They had no word for the specific weight of his question—a query about whether they intended to stay as guests or leave as ghosts. The silence that followed was heavy, filled with the things that couldn't be translated into any language.

The tension broke when Bjorn slumped back onto his bench. He looked at the ledger, then at his own calloused hands. “The math doesn't lie,” he muttered. It wasn't a surrender; it was an acknowledgment of the harsh reality Hallvard had laid bare.

“We leave,” Eirik said. The words were final. They didn't need to be shouted. 

A long, hollow silence followed—the sound of men accepting a hard truth. Then came the movement of hands. 

It began with Bjorn. He stood and approached the strangers. From his pack, he pulled a piece of seasoned wood—a block of the very timber they had argued over, shaped into a sturdy handle, polished until it shone like amber. He held it out.

The stranger took it. His fingers were long and steady. In return, he reached into a pouch at his belt and produced a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth. He handed it to Bjorn. 

Neither man spoke of what the items represented. There were no names for these exchanges in the shared vocabulary of the camp. They were simply pieces of value traded between two people who understood that survival required more than just food; it required a recognition of one another’s existence.

Hallvard watched this exchange with a strange ache in his chest. He felt like an observer of a ritual he didn't fully comprehend, yet he understood the gravity of it perfectly.

As the men began to move about, preparing to strike the camp and gather what could be carried, Eirik stood and walked toward Hallvard. He stopped just outside the fire’s reach. 

Between them lay the knotted cord—the one Hallvard had carried since the first week of winter. It was a tangled mess of hemp and twine, something they had both touched but never discussed. 

Eirik reached down and picked it up. He held it for a moment, his thumb tracing the complex series of knots. 

“It is a tally,” Eirik said softly. His voice was thick with the exhaustion of the season. “Each knot is a day we survived when the ice tried to crack the hull. Each knot is a meal we shared when there was only enough for half the men.”

He handed it back to Hallvard. 

“It’s too late to use it as a map,” Eirik added, a ghost of a grim smile touching his lips. “But it's good to know what we did.”

Hallvard took the cord. The reconciliation wasn't in words; it was in the shared weight of the history they had just written into that ledger. They were leaving this place, and the land would eventually forget their names, but the knots remained.

Outside, the wind shifted again, carrying the scent of salt and moving water. The thaw had begun. 

Hallvard looked at the last page of his ledger—the one he had left unfinished. He picked up his charcoal and made a single mark: *Departure.*

## Chapter 10: Homeward Is Not the Same

The gray of the Greenland coast did not rise to meet them with a flourish. It bled into the horizon, a smudge of charcoal and bruised slate against the churning white of the surf. 

Hallvard stood at the rail of the *Sjofar*, his hands gripping the salt-crusted wood until his knuckles turned the color of bleached bone. The wind was a wheezing thing today, carrying the scent of wet earth and old snow—the smell of home. Or at least, the home they had left behind to find something more.

The ship groaned as it crested a swell, the timber hull complaining in a language Hallvard understood better than his own mother tongue. He felt the weight of the voyage in the slump of his shoulders. It was a heaviness that didn't come from the cargo, though the hold was bursting with seasoned timber and barrels of salt fish. It was a weight of miles, of nights spent watching the stars shift into unfamiliar patterns, and of the way the silence of the open sea had slowly eroded the noise of his own thoughts.

"Land!" someone shouted. The cry was thin, swallowed by the gale.

Hallvard didn't cheer. He simply watched the jagged teeth of the coastline bite into the water. Brusand appeared—a squat, stubborn thing of rock and moss. It looked smaller than it had in his dreams, less magnificent than the maps had promised. It was just land. It was solid, and it was tired, and they were tired.

The unloading began before the anchor had even fully bitten into the seabed. 

It was a choreography of muscle and desperation. Men moved like shadows against the gray light, their faces masked by soot and salt. Hallvard stood on the pier, his ledger tucked firmly under one arm, watching the transition from sea to shore. They hauled the timber first—thick beams of seasoned wood that smelled of resin and deep forests. The wood was heavy, slick with brine, requiring three men to heave each length onto the carts.

"Easy on the lash!" Hallvard barked, his voice rasping. "We don't want to crack the grain."

He watched a group of locals move among the ships. They were lean people, their furs matted with grease, moving with a deliberate, economical grace that made the Norsemen look like bumbling giants by comparison. They didn't watch the newcomers with awe; they looked at them as one might look at a stubborn herd of cattle—something to be managed, moved, and integrated into the rhythm of the season. They worked the heavy winch together, their hands steady on the ropes, their faces set in expressions of focused neutrality.

Among the crew, a small boy emerged from the shadows of the main mast. He was shivering, his tunic soaked through, but he stood upright. 

"He did it," the boy whispered to Hallvard as they passed. "He stayed under. He swam the length of the bay."

Hallvard looked at the lad—a scrap of a thing who had been terrified of the deep water three months ago. Now, the boy’s eyes held a hard, glassy sheen. The sea had taken its toll and given him a gift in return. It was a small victory, a tiny tally to be kept in the mind but not on the page.

By the time the sun began to dip, casting long, distorted shadows across the wharf, the bulk of the cargo was secured. The *Sjofar* sat lower in the water, its belly full of the things they had traded for—the heavy, honest weight of survival. 

Hallvard retreated to his small cabin, a space that smelled of damp wool and old ink. He sat at the small table, his lamp flickering against the encroaching gloom. He pulled the ledger from its leather wrap. It was a thick book, its pages swollen with the history of a dozen voyages. 

He opened it to the final entry. He took his quill and dipped it into the inkpot. 

*Voyage to the West,* he began. His hand hesitated.

He looked at the columns. They were neat, precise lines: *Grain (Bushels), Wool (Weight), Iron (Units), Salt (Pounds).* He tried to find a place for the way the light had changed in the middle of the ocean—a hue of blue so deep it felt like falling into a well. He tried to find a column for the silence that had settled over the crew, a silence that wasn't just an absence of noise, but a presence of something else. 

He tried to record the way the Indigenous people moved through the mist, their movements fluid and purposeful, a life lived in harmony with the frost. He tried to find a number for the fear he had felt when the horizon vanished, leaving only the ship and the void.

The ink bled into the paper, forming a dark, ugly blotch. 

He stared at it for a long time. The ledger was a tool of commerce, a way to turn the world into manageable units. But the voyage hadn't been a transaction. It had been an undoing. He realized that if he wrote down what they had seen, the words would be lies, because no word in their tongue could capture the weight of it.

He closed the book with a dull thud. He left the last page entirely blank. 

Outside, the wind died down, leaving a heavy, oppressive stillness. The sound of boots on timber announced someone at the door. It was Eirik. The man looked older than he had when they had departed; his beard was shot through with gray, and his eyes seemed to have retreated into his skull.

"The men are asking," Eirik said. He didn't enter the cabin, staying in the doorway where the shadows gathered. "They see the timber is moved. They see the fish is salted. They think the story is finished."

Hallvard looked at the ledger on the table. "The story isn't finished. It just doesn't fit in the book."

Eirik leaned against the doorframe, his hands tucked into his furs. "They want to know if we go back out. If we turn the prow toward the setting sun again when the ice breaks."

Hallvard felt a phantom ache in his chest, the ghost of a compass needle spinning wildly. He thought of the blank page. He thought of the boy who could now swim in the freezing deep. 

"What did you tell them?" Hallvard asked.

Eirik gave a short, dry laugh that ended in a cough. "I told them the truth, as best as a man can tell it."

"And what is that?"

"I told them to ask me in a year," Eirik said. "The sea doesn't give up its secrets easily. We need to let the salt dry on our skin first."

Hallvard nodded. He reached out and touched the leather cover of the ledger. 

He stood and walked to the small chest in the corner. From it, he pulled the chart—the map they had used to navigate the unknown. It was stained with water and grease, its edges frayed. He looked at it for a moment, then placed it back into the chest, tucking it deep beneath his spare blankets. 

The sea was behind them now. For now. 

He stepped out onto the wharf. The air was biting, smelling of woodsmoke and the sharp, metallic tang of the coming frost. In the distance, the Indigenous people were moving toward their fires, their silhouettes sharp against the dying light. They were going home to their hearths; Hallvard was going back to his ledger, which remained open, its last page a white desert of unrecorded things.
